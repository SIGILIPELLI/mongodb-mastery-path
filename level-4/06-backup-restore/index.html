    06 · Backup, Restore & Point-in-Time Recovery¶

    Replication is not a backup. A replica set faithfully copies every change to every member — including the deleteMany({}) someone ran against production instead of staging, which reaches all secondaries within milliseconds. A backup is a copy from before the mistake, plus a way to get from that copy to the moment you actually need.

    Level 1 · 09 introduced mongodump and mongorestore for moving data around. This lesson uses them as a backup system on a live replica set: taking a consistent dump while transactions are running, proving why the oplog matters by restoring without it, recovering to the exact second before a destructive command, and taking a file-level copy. Tools: MongoDB 9.0.2 and Database Tools 100.19.1, on the three-node set from Lesson 01.

    The test: an invariant that must survive¶

    A bank.accounts collection holds 500,000 accounts with a balance of 1,000 each. A Python writer moves 7 units between random accounts inside transactions, so the total must always be exactly 500,000,000. Any backup that restores to a different total captured a state that never existed.

    while time.time() < end:
    x, y = random.sample(range(500000), 2)
    with c.start_session() as s:
        s.with_transaction(lambda s: (a.update_one({"_id": x}, {"$inc": {"balance": -7}}, session=s),
                                      a.update_one({"_id": y}, {"$inc": {"balance": 7}}, session=s)))

    Dumping a live replica set with --oplog¶

    With the writer running, dump from a secondary (keeping the load off the primary):

    mongodump --uri "mongodb://127.0.0.1:27301,127.0.0.1:27302,127.0.0.1:27303/?replicaSet=rs4" \
          --readPreference secondary --oplog --out dump2

    done dumping `bank.accounts` (500000 documents)
writing captured oplog to ``
    dumped 812 oplog entries
writer transfers: 1216

    A dump is not instantaneous: it reads each collection with a cursor while writes continue, so different documents are copied at different moments. --oplog fixes that. Before starting, mongodump notes the newest oplog timestamp; when it finishes, it also copies every oplog entry written during the dump into oplog.bson. Replaying those entries on restore rolls every document forward to the same moment — the end of the dump.

    Restoring the same dump into two empty servers, once without and once with replay:

    mongorestore --port 27601 --nsInclude 'bank.*' dump2
mongorestore --port 27602 --oplogReplay dump2

    500000 document(s) restored successfully. 0 document(s) failed to restore.
applied 1624 oplog entries
27601 total balance: 499999958
27602 total balance: 500000000

    Without replay, the restored bank is 42 units short: the dump caught six transfers half-way, with one side copied before the transfer and the other after. With replay, the total is exact. (812 oplog entries became 1,624 applied operations because each transaction's entry carries both of its updates.)

    Honesty note: the first attempt used 50,000 accounts, and that dump finished in 0.4 seconds — both restores balanced, because no transfer happened to straddle a copied pair. A small test passing doesn't prove consistency; the larger dump did.

    Two operational details:

    • --oplog works only on a whole-instance dump (no --db/--collection), and the restore refuses filters with replay: Failed: cannot use --oplogReplay with includes specified. Restore everything to a scratch server, then copy out what you need.
    • --oplog gives a consistent snapshot of a replica set. For a sharded cluster, mongodump through mongos can't produce a cluster-wide consistent point; sharded clusters need coordinated snapshots (managed services, Ops Manager, or careful file-level procedures).

    Point-in-time recovery¶

    The dump finished at 18:44:43 UTC. Life goes on — 309 more transfers — and then:

    db.getSiblingDB("bank").accounts.deleteMany({_id: {$lt: 1000}})

    OOPS deleted: 1000
writer transfers: 311
live now: 499000 docs, total 498999993

    Restoring last night's dump would lose every transfer since. What you want is the database as it was one operation before the delete. That's the dump plus the oplog up to that operation.

    Step 1: find the bad operation in the oplog. Note where the dump's own oplog ends, then search after it. In this version a multi-document delete is written as applyOps batches, so search inside them:

    // last entry of dump2/oplog.bson: {'$timestamp': {'t': 1791830683, 'i': 93}}
db.getSiblingDB("local").oplog.rs.find({ts: {$gt: Timestamp(1791830683, 93)}, "o.applyOps.op": "d"})
  .sort({$natural: 1}).limit(3)

    1791830713:1 c admin.$cmd ops in entry: 10 first: {"_id":9}  lsid? false 2026-10-12T18:45:13.472Z
1791830713:2 c admin.$cmd ops in entry: 10 first: {"_id":19} lsid? false 2026-10-12T18:45:13.473Z
1791830713:3 c admin.$cmd ops in entry: 10 first: {"_id":29} lsid? false 2026-10-12T18:45:13.473Z
delete-bearing entries: 100

    The delete begins at timestamp 1791830713:1 — 100 entries of 10 deletes each. In a real incident you'd narrow the search by wall time ("around 18:45 UTC") and by namespace.

    Step 2: save the oplog since the dump — before it rolls over, since the oplog is a fixed-size buffer:

    mongodump --host rs4/127.0.0.1:27301,127.0.0.1:27302 --readPreference secondary -d local -c oplog.rs \
  --query '{"ts": {"$gt": {"$timestamp": {"t": 1791830683, "i": 93}}}}' --out oplogdump
mkdir replay && cp oplogdump/local/oplog.rs.bson replay/oplog.bson

    done dumping `local.oplog.rs` (1046 documents)

    Step 3: restore the dump to a scratch server, then replay up to — not including — the bad timestamp:

    mongorestore --port 27603 --oplogReplay dump2
mongorestore --port 27603 --oplogReplay --oplogLimit 1791830713:1 replay

    applied 1624 oplog entries
501679 document(s) restored successfully. 0 document(s) failed to restore.
replaying oplog
applied 1236 oplog entries
recovered: 500000 docs, total 500000000 | _id<1000 present: 1000

    All 500,000 accounts, the exact total, and every transfer up to the moment of the delete. --oplogLimit replays entries strictly before the given timestamp.

    Step 4: put the lost documents back, copying only them from the scratch server to production:

    mongodump --port 27603 -d bank -c accounts --query '{"_id": {"$lt": 1000}}' --out fix
mongorestore --uri "$PROD_URI" fix

    done dumping `bank.accounts` (1000 documents)
1000 document(s) restored successfully. 0 document(s) failed to restore.
live after putting them back: 500000 docs, total 499999993

    Every account is back — and production is 7 units short. Between the delete and the fix, a transfer touched one of the deleted accounts: update_one on a missing document matched nothing and silently did nothing, while the other half of the transfer succeeded. Restoring data cannot undo what applications did because data was missing. The real fix was in the writer — check matched_count and abort the transaction if it isn't 1 — and the recovery runbook should include reconciling anything written during the incident window.

    File-level copies¶

    For large databases, copying data files is much faster than dumping documents. The files must be consistent; on a secondary you can briefly stop writes to disk with fsyncLock:

    db.fsyncLock()      // on a secondary: flush and block writes

    cp -R rs/n1 snap    # copy the whole dbpath

    db.fsyncUnlock()

    Long('1')
unlock lockCount: Long('0')
locked for 1.4s
73M
snapshot copy (standalone): 500000 docs, total 499999993
still has replset config: 1

    The copy started as a standalone server with all data. Notes:

    • Copy the entire dbpath, including WiredTiger*, journal/, and every collection-*/index-* file. A partial copy is unusable.
    • While locked, the secondary falls behind; keep it short, and never lock the primary.
    • The copy still contains the replica set config in local. To use it as a seed for a new member that's fine; to use it as an independent server, start it without --replSet (as here).
    • Filesystem or volume snapshots (LVM, ZFS, cloud disk snapshots) are the production form of this technique. If the journal is on the same volume, an atomic snapshot is consistent even without fsyncLock because WiredTiger recovers from its journal at startup, just as after a crash.

    Choosing a strategy¶

    Method Good for Weak at
    mongodump --oplog Small-to-medium replica sets; logical copies portable across versions Slow for large data; restore rebuilds every index; not consistent for sharded clusters
    Volume snapshots Large data; fast restore Tied to storage; must cover the whole dbpath atomically
    Oplog archiving + base backup Point-in-time recovery to any second Must keep the oplog archive continuous — a gap breaks the chain
    Managed backups (Atlas, Ops Manager) Sharded clusters, scheduled PITR Not available in a plain Community install; not demonstrated here

    Whatever you use, the rule is the same as for every database: a backup you haven't restored is a hope, not a backup. Schedule restore tests, measure how long they take (that's your real recovery time), and check invariants like the bank total — not just that the restore "succeeded".

    How It Actually Works¶

    mongodump is an ordinary client: it lists collections and reads each with a cursor, writing BSON to files and index definitions to metadata JSON. With --oplog it first reads the newest oplog entry's timestamp, dumps collections, then queries local.oplog.rs for everything after that starting timestamp and writes it to oplog.bson. Each document in the dump reflects some moment between start and end. Because oplog entries are idempotent (Lesson 01) — "set balance to 993", not "subtract 7" — replaying entries over documents that already include some of those changes is harmless, and every document ends at the state as of the last replayed entry. Point-in-time recovery is the same mechanism with more oplog and a stop point.

    mongorestore inserts documents in batches, then builds indexes from the metadata (index builds dominate restore time for large collections), then — with --oplogReplay — applies the entries with applyOps. A file copy skips all of that: WiredTiger's files are the database. At startup it finds the last checkpoint, then replays its journal to recover anything written after it, which is why a crash-consistent snapshot of the whole dbpath restores cleanly.

    Common mistakes¶

    • Treating replicas as backups. They replicate the mistake too.
    • Dumping a busy replica set without --oplog, or restoring without --oplogReplay, producing states that never existed.
    • An oplog window shorter than your detection time, so the operations you need to replay are already gone. Archive the oplog continuously if you need PITR.
    • Never testing restores, or testing only that the tool exits successfully.
    • File copies of a running primary without a lock or atomic snapshot.
    • Restoring over production instead of into a scratch server and copying out what's needed.
    • Forgetting side effects during the incident window — writes that failed or did nothing because data was missing.

    Exercise¶

    1. Reproduce the invariant test: dump a busy bank.accounts without --oplog and with it, restore both, and compare totals. Make the collection large enough that the difference shows up.
    2. Write a script pitr.sh <dump_dir> <limit_ts> <scratch_port> that performs steps 2 and 3 automatically. Test it by recovering from an accidental updateMany({}, {$set: {balance: 0}}).
    3. Fix the writer so that a transfer involving a missing account aborts its transaction, rerun the incident, and show the total is exact after the fix.
    4. Measure: how long do mongodump and mongorestore take for your 500,000 accounts, and how much of the restore is index building? Add two secondary indexes and measure again.
    5. Write a nightly job outline that keeps a dump plus a continuous oplog archive, with a retention policy and a monthly automated restore test that checks an invariant.
